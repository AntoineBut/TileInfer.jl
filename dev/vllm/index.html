Comparison with vLLM

TileInfer against vLLM 0.31 on the same machine (RTX 4080 SUPER), in the setup one would run for real: Qwen3-4B with DFlash speculative decoding (k = 15), with BF16 weights and with INT8 weights. What each of TileInfer's features gains on its own is in Results by feature.

Setup

  • vLLM: CUDA graphs and torch.compile, one sequence at a time, prefix caching off. Its INT8 runs use a W8A16 compressed-tensors copy of the checkpoint quantized as TileInfer's quantize_int8 (every linear and the lm_head), on its Marlin kernels.
  • Workload: the two tasks of bench/prompts.jl on Julia source code, renaming a type throughout it (which copies the input) and explaining it (open text), greedy, stopping at EOS, with the same token ids for both engines. Two points:
    • 1k: 1,024-token prompts (1,021 and 1,018 tokens), 128 tokens, the reference point of Results by feature;
    • 8k: 8,192-token prompts (8,181 and 8,178 tokens), 2,048 tokens.
  • Metric: per prompt, the median latency of the reply and of a 1-token request ("prefill") over 5 trials after a 1 s warm-up; decode = (n − 1) / (latency(n) − latency(1)). Each configuration runs in fresh processes (3 at 1k, 2 at 8k), which agree within 1 %.
  • Scripts: bench/ref/ (vllm_spec.py, tileinfer_spec.jl, run_all.sh; setup in its README).

The replies start with the same tokens, but in BF16 the two engines' drafts are accepted differently (presumably rounding differences between the engines' kernels; not investigated): which engine wins follows the tokens per step. With INT8 weights both take the same 13 and 43 steps; vLLM's step is then 7 % cheaper on the edit and the same on the explanation. INT8 also changes the replies after a few tokens, which happens to make the edit's drafts longer (9.8 tokens per step against 7.1).

8k prompts

The explanations end before 2,048 tokens, at their EOS. 8k is about half the longest prompt TileInfer takes with DFlash in BF16 (~13k tokens): it prefills the prompt in one step, and the drafter then reads its hidden states in one step of its own, ~400 KB of buffers per prompt token next to 167 KB of KV caches, where the caches alone would fit ~32k positions. vLLM prefills in chunks.

  • Drafts are accepted far less: the edit takes 1.87 tokens per step at 8k against 7.1 at 1k, on both engines, with the same tokens (1,093 and 1,096 steps for 2,048 tokens): it comes from the drafter, not from an engine (why is not investigated).
  • TileInfer's steps are cheaper, by their attention: with the same steps, a step (drafter pass and verify) costs 19.6 ms against vLLM's 26.9 in BF16 (1.37×; INT8 1.53×), where a plain decode step at 1k costs 12.7. Profiled (bench/ref/profile.sh, 256 tokens after the 8k prompts), the GEMMs cost the same (15.1 ms against 15.4 ms of GPU time per step) and neither engine leaves the GPU idle (busy 98.5 % and 99.8 % of the step), but the verify step's attention takes 9.0 ms in vLLM against 2.1 ms: its FlashAttention flash_fwd_splitkv_kernel runs one block per query head (grid 1 × 1 × 32, no split of the 8k positions over the 80 SMs), 250 µs per layer (~135 GB/s), where TileInfer's decode attention splits the positions in 8 (64 blocks, 56 µs per layer, ~600 GB/s). The gap grows with the context. Prefix caching plays no part: it is off in these runs. On Ada vLLM runs FlashAttention 2, whose varlen path (the only one vLLM calls) takes no split count; its Triton backend (--attention-backend TRITON_ATTN) splits only one-token steps, and costs the same here (10.3 ms of attention per step); FlashInfer's JIT build fails in this environment (nvcc 13.4 against CUDA 13.0 headers). More splits do not help TileInfer's kernel either: 16, 32 or 64 splits are as fast or slower than 8 at 1k to 16k positions (the Float32 partials of 64 query rows per split add traffic), and 4 are 1.6× slower.
  • Prefill ties (~9.3k tok/s in BF16, against 12k at 1k: attention grows with the prompt).
